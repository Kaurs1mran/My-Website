Sikh Empire to Independence

This page picks up where my Sikh History page left off, from the rise of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's empire through the fall of Sikh rule, the British era, independence, and a brief look at what came after.

The Sikh Empire

Out of the twelve misls came a leader who would unite them all. Ranjit Singh, born in 1780, captured Lahore in 1799 at just 19 and declared himself Maharaja of Punjab by 1801, uniting the misls into a single Sikh Empire that stretched from the Khyber Pass to the Sutlej River. His army, the Khalsa Fauj, included Sikhs, Muslims, and Hindus, and he brought in European officers, including a few of Napoleon's former generals, to modernize it. Major campaigns under his rule included the conquest of Multan in 1818, Kashmir in 1819, and Peshawar in 1834, pushing the empire's frontier all the way to the edge of Afghanistan.

He was known for his respect for all faiths, donating gold to cover the Golden Temple, but also giving even more gold to Kashi Vishwanath Temple in Benares and generously funding mosques across his empire.

His most famous general, Hari Singh Nalwa, is still widely regarded as one of the greatest military commanders in history. Historian K.M. Panikkar called him "the noblest and most gallant of the Sikh generals of his time, the very embodiment of honour, chivalry, and courage."

After Ranjit Singh's death in 1839, internal succession conflicts weakened the empire, and Punjab became the last major independent kingdom in India to fall, only after the Anglo-Sikh Wars. When the British annexed Punjab in 1849, they took the Koh-i-Noor diamond as part of the settlement, a jewel that remains in the British Crown Jewels today.

Ranjit Singh's youngest son, Duleep Singh, became Maharaja at just five years old after his father's death, but was separated from his mother and sent to England at 15 by the British, where he was raised Christian and became a favorite of Queen Victoria, who nicknamed him "the Black Prince."

Sikhs, the British, and Independence

After the Anglo-Sikh Wars, the British came to respect the fighting ability of Sikh soldiers enough that they began recruiting them directly into the British Indian Army. One of the most famous stories from this era is the Battle of Saragarhi in 1897, where just 21 Sikh soldiers held off an attacking force of around 10,000 Afghan tribesmen, fighting to the last man rather than surrender. In both World Wars, Sikh soldiers served in huge numbers relative to their population, over 83,000 were killed and more than 100,000 wounded across the two wars, and Sikh regiments remain among the most decorated in military history.

At the same time, many Sikhs were fighting against British rule, not for it. The Ghadar Party, founded by Punjabi immigrants in North America, organized early armed resistance, with figures like Kartar Singh Sarabha, who was hanged at just 19 years old and later became a personal hero to revolutionary Bhagat Singh. Many of the victims of the 1919 Jallianwala Bagh massacre in Amritsar were Sikh, and the event became a turning point in the wider independence movement.

The Akali movement of the 1920s challenged British authority through organized, largely nonviolent resistance over control of Sikh gurdwara management, and later Sikh soldiers made up more than 60% of the roughly 20,000 who joined the Indian National Army, which fought against the British during World War II.

Sikhs made up less than 2% of India's population at the time, yet writer Kartar Singh Duggal recorded that 93 of the 121 freedom fighters hanged by the British, and over 2,000 of those sentenced to life imprisonment, were Sikh. Broader claims that Sikhs made up 70-90% of all freedom fighters are widely repeated but disputed, since reliable, complete records from the era are hard to come by, still, by any account, their contribution was far out of proportion to their small share of the population.

After Independence

Partition in 1947 split Punjab itself in two, between India and Pakistan, tearing apart a region that had been one for centuries. Even after that, Punjab within India was divided again in 1966, after years of the Punjabi Suba movement, before Sikhs got a Punjabi-majority state of their own.

Tensions between the Sikh community and the Indian government continued for decades after, culminating in Operation Blue Star in 1984, a military assault on the Golden Temple complex, and the violence against Sikhs in Delhi and other cities that followed later that year. Different people describe what happened differently, some call it riots, others call it a genocide, and I don't think either way of seeing it is wrong. This is a much longer, heavier chapter than I can properly cover here, so I'll leave it at that for now.

Timeline of Major Battles

Year Battle Outcome
1704 Battle of Chamkaur Around 40 Sikhs, including two of Guru Gobind Singh's sons, held off a vastly larger Mughal force
1710 Battle of Sirhind Banda Singh Bahadur avenged the Sahibzade, defeating the governor of Sirhind
1748 Battle of Sodhra and Badra 2,000 Sikh fighters defeated over 12,000 Mughal cavalry and infantry
1761 Third Battle of Panipat Sikh horsemen freed thousands of captured Maratha women in the aftermath
1762 Vadda Ghallughara Tens of thousands of Sikhs killed in a single day; the misls rebuilt within a year
1799 Capture of Lahore Ranjit Singh, at just 19, captured Lahore and began uniting the misls
1807 Battle of Kasur Ranjit Singh defeated Afghan chief Qutb-ud-Din, expanding toward Afghanistan
1813 Battle of Attock Ranjit Singh's forces defeated Afghan troops, extending Sikh territory
1818 Conquest of Multan Ranjit Singh's forces captured Multan, ending Afghan control there
1819 Battle of Shopian Kashmir annexed into the Sikh Empire
1834 Conquest of Peshawar Hari Singh Nalwa captured Peshawar, extending the empire to the Hindu Kush
1837 Battle of Jamrud Hari Singh Nalwa held the western frontier but was killed in the fighting
1845-1846 First Anglo-Sikh War Sikh Empire defeated; lost territory east of the Sutlej River
1848-1849 Second Anglo-Sikh War British annex Punjab entirely, ending the Sikh Empire

1984 Operation Blue Star Military assault on the Golden Temple complex; led to lasting tension and violence
